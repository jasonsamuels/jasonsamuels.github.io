This post is both the final reading summary for this blog, and a reflection on the reading summary creation process, why I did that during my 2017-18 nonprofit certificate pursuit, and why I abandoned it during my 2024-25 MPA completion.

## About the reading summaries, generally

Way back in the [first one of these that I produced](https://lol.jasonsamuels.net/readings-sp-wk-1-2-3-f17/) I wrote that my intent was to write short synopses of class readings to reflect on what was covered in them, for the purpose of better understanding the material. My method felt innovative at the time - I did all my reading on a Microsoft Surface using a Surface Pen to highlight as I went, then synced those files to a PC where I'd go back through them and copy-paste out all the highlighted passages, using keyboard macros to speed up my workflow. As I did that I would alternate between copy-pasting key points and weaving those points together into a more coherent outline, picking and highlighting especially resonant points among that. Later when assignments were due, my paper writing process usually involved drawing from those highlighted passages for the paper outlines. 

Looking back at that process close to a decade later, I know that when getting back to grad school this practice combined with my stubborn streak to help keep me accountable to doing all of the readings, multiple times. I couldn't have gaps in my outlines. That served me well academically, and it genuinely helped me learn the material. I wanted to get the most out of the academic experience, and what I got was a grounding in knowledge and concepts that have positively shaped me and my approach to work ever since. But the exhaustive practice was grueling, it took a toll, and years later when I went back to complete my master's I decided to let this blog go. I still did all the readings, just not multiple times, and my tooling evolved to marking up readings via a reMarkable paper tablet, then syncing that through the PC app to a PDF export for working through highlighted passages to produce content outlines.

## About this reading summary

The reading summary below was created with AI assistance, specifically Claude Opus 5 by Anthropic. It started with [my marked-up copy of the encyclical letter](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1ZR5fcs-eFG7kdoIixqdZP1NDXuOzLZjq/view?usp=sharing), and a prompt to model a draft post based on my existing reading summaries. I told it specifically to just copy-paste all highlighted text into a bulleted list, and preserve the passage number it came from at the beginning of each line. Then I re-prompted to combine bullet points that came from the same passage, and to help fill in the opening 39 passages where there is no highlighting because that's the point in reading it where I thought to pick up my highlighter. Then I took a pass through the outline, making minor edits while selecting and highlighting especially resonant points. After my editing pass, I turned that back to the AI, told it to analyze my edits and make similar light paraphrasing edits throughout the outline, and generate one-paragraph overviews of each chapter based on my highlights. A last AI pass cleaned up punctuation and grammar, then the final edits are mine.

I chose to summarize this document here because I believe it is remarkably relevant to our time, and that it is one that historians of the future will scrutinize. This letter is far more than a moral statement on technological progress, it's a critique of evils that plague humanity - greed, inequality, intolerance, war. It's neither pro nor anti technology, but rather an exploration of its perils and potential. Most of all it is a call to defend and cherish humanity in an era of rapid change where technocratic forces threaten to further strip humanity from systems that hold power over humans.

None of the words in this post so far were machine-drafted. That part begins now...

## Introduction

Leo XIV opens by refusing both the boosterish and the apocalyptic framings of AI, and he sets the stakes as a choice rather than a forecast. The emphasis falls on dehumanization as an old temptation now wearing a technical guise, and on the duty that follows from it — to remain profoundly human at precisely the moment when the tools make that harder. Technology is not the antagonist here; the question is who holds its power and toward what end.

* 1 - Humanity, created by God in all its grandeur, is today facing a pivotal choice; yet every era also runs the risk of creating an inhumane and more unjust world
* 2 - Openness to dialogue is an integral part of the Church’s vocation
* 3 - The proclamation of the Gospel cannot overlook the concrete lives of people; Social Doctrine offers principles for thought, criteria for discernment and judgment, and concrete guidelines for action

### The res novae of our time

* 4 - Technology should not be considered, in itself, as a force antagonistic to humanity; each phase of progress has revealed the ambiguity of tools that can cause harm when not oriented toward the good
* 5 - The issue is not limited to regulation; Technological power thus takes on an unprecedented, predominantly “private” aspect
* 6 - If we focus only on contingencies, we risk letting the succession of emergencies dictate the direction of our path.

### Two biblical images

* 9 - Technology in and of itself is not a solution to humanity’s problems, just as it is not inherently evil; technology is never neutral, because it takes on the characteristics of those who devise, finance, regulate and use it
* 10 - **The risk of dehumanization — of building a future that excludes God and reduces the other to a means — is an ancient and ever-new temptation that today takes on a technical guise.**

### Building for the common good

* 12 - Building for the common good means accepting the limits and weakness of humanity without considering them an error to be corrected; true fulfilment is not achieved by eliminating weakness but through harmonious growth
* 13 - The logic of subsidiarity values the cooperation between generations, peoples, disciplines and cultures
* 14 - We cannot condone naïve enthusiasms, nor fuel unfounded fears; standards for discernment: the dignity of the human person, the universal destination of goods, the preferential option for the poor, care for our common home and peace

### Remaining human

* 15 - In the era of artificial intelligence, **when human dignity is threatened by new forms of dehumanization, ours is the pressing duty to remain profoundly human.**

## Chapter one - A dynamic approach faithful to the Gospel

A walk through Social Doctrine from Rerum Novarum forward, establishing that this is a tradition that develops rather than a fixed code. The line carrying the weight is that truth is not a territory to be defended but a good to be shared, and it sets the posture for everything after: the Church arrives at AI with a process of shared discernment rather than a handbook of answers.

* 17 - Artificial intelligence, too, should not be considered as merely yet another theme to be studied or a crisis to be managed, but rather as a development that challenges the categories of Social Doctrine from within
* 18 - The Church walks alongside humanity, recognizing the autonomy of earthly realities and the distinction between ecclesial and political communities

### A Church journeying through human history

* 19 - The Church cannot consider herself a stranger to the forces shaping society
* 20 - The Church thus stands alongside the world without overpowering it
* 21 - The Church does not claim to assume the functions belonging to the State.
* 22 - Listening to the “many voices” is no mere sociological exercise, but instead requires spiritual discernment.

### The wisdom of the word of God in dialogue with the human sciences

* 23 - The Church is not afraid to encounter human knowledge
* 24 - Social Doctrine does not amount to a repertoire of technical solutions or an economic or political model to be set against others

### Social Doctrine as a shared discernment

* 25 - **Truth is not a territory to be defended, but a good to be shared**
* 26 - It is unrealistic to think that the Church’s Social Doctrine can propose a single response that is valid in all contexts
* 27 - It is not a handbook of principles and norms to be applied, but a process of shared discernment.

### The first stages of the Church’s Social Doctrine

* 30 - Rerum Novarum recognizes that persons have a fundamental value that takes precedence over capital and profit; the primacy of human labor over any mindset focused solely on finance or productivity
* 31 - Quadragesimo Anno denounces the concentration of economic power in the hands of a few; whatever can be carried out by individuals, families, intermediary organizations and local communities should not be carried out by higher-level authorities; injustice concerns not only individual behavior but also economic and institutional structures
* 32 - The need for law to take precedence over interests; economic disparities are a breeding ground for tension and violence

### The years of the Second Vatican Council

* 33 - Lasting peace requires institutions and relations between peoples that are inspired by the dignity of every person
* 34 - Economic and institutional structures are just only to the extent that they serve the integral development of the person
* 35 - Development is a transition from less humane to more humane living conditions
* 36 - The criteria for recognizing what humanizes or dehumanizes and what liberates or oppresses in ever-changing situations; no person or people will be treated as expendable in the processes of development

### The recent Magisterium

* 37 - Fair wages reveal whether the worker is treated as a person or merely as a cost of production; Work is not considered simply as a problem to be dealt with or a means of generating income, but a fundamental good for the person
* 38 - Economic, financial and commercial mechanisms, managed by the strongest economies, structurally favor their own interests while stifling weaker economies; solidarity was understood as a concrete, shared responsibility among individuals, peoples and nations
* 39 - The positive potential of the market and private initiative holds only if they remain subordinate to the moral law and are guided by the principle of solidarity, without sacrificing the most vulnerable to the rationale of profit.

## Chapter two - Foundations and principles of the Social Doctrine of the Church

The five principles are named as the working toolkit for the AI question — common good, universal destination of goods, subsidiarity, solidarity, social justice. What gets stressed is their extension into new territory: the universal destination of goods now reaching past material things to data, algorithms and patents; solidarity as a decision rather than a sentiment; treatment of the least as the actual measure; and a digital-age definition of a just social order. ¶85 supplies the test the rest of the document keeps returning to — do these changes make people more humane and fraternal?

* 46 - **In order to protect the human person in the age of artificial intelligence, I believe that today we must once again reflect on the common good, the universal destination of goods, subsidiarity, solidarity and social justice.**

### The equal dignity of all human beings

* 51 - It is important to ensure that this growth in appreciation of human dignity is not obscured by the pressure of new ideologies or very powerful interests in today’s world. Among these ideologies, I consider particularly insidious the one that suggests that every person must earn or justify his or her own worth, to the point of attributing greater value to those who are more efficient or effective.

### The supreme value of human rights

* 58 - Similarly, it is not enough to extol individual freedom or private enterprise if we then allow a multitude of people to continue living without decent work, protections or access to basic necessities.

### The principle of the common good

* 59 - The first major principle of Social Doctrine that I wish to highlight: the common good.
* 61 - It is an illusion to think that simply pursuing one’s own progress without caring for others is sufficient for contributing to the good of all.
* 62 - Working together for the common good means having a shared vision.
* 63 - **When politics abandons a long-term perspective and reduces itself to short-term calculations or sterile polarizations, then the language of the common good loses credibility, and, at the same time, social inequalities and divisions grow.**

### The principle of the universal destination of goods

* 65 - This principle reminds us that **the earth’s goods — soil, water, air and natural resources — are given by God to the entire human family to sustain the lives of all, and that every person has an inherent right to the use of such goods**; Today, we are called to recognize that **this universal destination applies not only to material goods, but also to immaterial and cultural goods.**
* 67 - Today, among the goods that are universally intended for everyone, we must also include new forms of property, such as patents, algorithms, digital platforms, technological infrastructure and data; the new possibilities offered by technology must be regulated in such a way as to respect the environment, avoid waste and prevent new forms of exploitation.

### The principle of subsidiarity

* 71 - The principle of subsidiarity applies especially in the context of the digital revolution.
* 72 - When it comes to decisions regarding economic flows and digital platforms, we cannot allow a handful of actors to dictate these processes on their own

### The principle of solidarity

* 73 - Solidarity is the concrete recognition that the future of each individual is connected to the future of all; there is an intimate link between subsidiarity and solidarity; this interconnectedness also pertains to the responsibility of authentic participation.
* 74 - **Solidarity arises precisely when we decide not to remain indifferent to what happens to our neighbor but instead to transform unavoidable bonds — economic, cultural and technological — into paths of sharing, cooperation and mutual care**
* 75 - The Church’s social teaching emphasizes that solidarity is both a principle and a virtue.
* 76 - Authentic development requires solidarity and inter-generational justice; Today, this responsibility also extends to digital and information infrastructure; Solidarity demands that decisions regarding data, algorithms, platforms and artificial intelligence take into account not only the immediate benefit for a few, but also the impact on all peoples and on future generations.

### The principle of social justice

* 77 - For the Christian community, social justice is a concrete way of following Jesus and remaining faithful to the Gospel; justice is born from, and fulfilled in, fraternity, because **the way we approach and relate to the least among us becomes, in concrete terms, the measure of our relationship with God**; Social justice is, therefore, characterized by the capacity of a social, economic and political order to allow everyone — particularly the weakest — to live a truly dignified life, without leaving anyone behind.
* 79 - Injustices do not arise solely from the wrong choices of individuals, but also from structures, mechanisms and economic and cultural systems that produce inequality almost automatically.
* 80 - **In the digital age, a just social order guarantees everyone equal access to opportunities, protects the youngest and weakest members of society, combats hate and misinformation and subjects the use of data and technology to public oversight**
* 81 - A litmus test for social justice today is the treatment of migrants; The way a society treats them reveals whether its sense of justice is driven by fear or by the spirit of fraternity.

### Integral human development

* 82 - By “integral human development,” we mean a process in which the growth of individuals and peoples encompasses all dimensions of existence and opens the future to subsequent generations as well.
* 83 - Development is truly human when it places people at the center; Development is not truly human if it increases consumption for some while shifting costs and burdens onto others
* 85 - Integral human development is the framework through which we can interpret the changes of our time, including those brought about by the digital revolution; technological innovations are not neutral; **the changes of our time must be evaluated by asking a crucial question: Do they truly help individuals and peoples to become more humane and fraternal, while respecting our common home and future generations?**

### An examen for the Church

* 86 - Social Doctrine is not merely a message; it is also an examination of conscience for the Church
* 89 - Living out justice in the Church means purifying ecclesial relationships and structures from distortions that give rise to inequality, lack of transparency and abuse of power.

## Chapter three - Technology and dominance. The grandeur of humanity in light of the promises of AI

The argument turns on two claims held together. First, AI is not morally neutral, and a more moral AI is not enough if a few determine whose morality it encodes — the technology amplifies whoever already holds resources, expertise and data. Second, the deeper danger is anthropological rather than technical: when efficiency becomes the ultimate measure, people start treating themselves as projects to be optimized. Against that, the chapter proposes care as the measure of a civilization, and accepts progress that relieves suffering so long as it does not cost us the capacity for relationship.

* 90 - As technological development rapidly transforms languages, relationships, institutions and forms of power, we believers must and can choose which projects to work on and in what manner, so as to safeguard and value the grandeur of humanity

### The technocratic paradigm and digital power

* 92 - Pope Francis denounced the growing dominance of a technocratic paradigm; tendency to let the logic of efficiency, control and profit alone shape personal, social and economic decisions; technology is not simply a tool; it begins to dictate what matters and what can be discarded, reducing creation to an object of exploitation and human beings to mere cogs in a system driven toward ever greater efficiency.
* 93 - Innovations can greatly serve integral human development and the care of our common home; because of their power, they can also hasten the expansion of the technocratic paradigm; More power does not necessarily imply something better.
* 94 - **The danger of humanity becoming a victim of its own achievements; technological progress requires careful discernment of the anthropological vision that guides it and the ends it pursues**; “having more” without “being more.”
* 95 - In many cases within the digital context, control over platforms, infrastructure, data and computing power does not rest with States, but with major economic and technological actors; When such power is concentrated in the hands of a few, it tends to become opaque and evade public oversight
* 96 - **Faced with this concentration of power, the criteria for judgment and discernment are the principles of Social Doctrine, inalienable dignity of the human, common good, universal destination of goods, subsidiarity, solidarity and social justice**; They demand that we assess whether the power of digital infrastructures and algorithms truly fosters participation and responsibility, protects the vulnerable, ensures fair access to opportunities and remains directed toward the good of all.

### Artificial intelligence

* 98 - I preface this discussion with two considerations; any statement regarding AI risks becoming quickly outdated; all of us, including those who design them, possess only a limited understanding of their actual functioning; an urgent need emerges for a twofold commitment: the deepening of scientific research and the exercise of moral and spiritual discernment.
* 99 - It is not possible to provide a single, comprehensive definition of AI; **avoid the misconception of equating this type of “intelligence” with that of human beings; systems merely imitate certain functions of human intelligence; often surpass human intelligence in speed and computational capacity; They may imitate language, behavior and analytical skills; but they do not understand what they produce**

### A valuable tool that requires vigilance

* 100 - AI can be a valuable tool and, at the same time, calls for a measured and vigilant approach; In personal use, three aspects in particular deserve careful consideration: the ease with which results are obtained, the impression of objectivity and the simulation of human communication; apparent objectivity can lead us to overlook the cultural assumptions of those who designed and trained them; When words are simulated, they do not build genuine relationships, but only their appearance.
* 101 - Broadening our perspective to the use of AI in society, gains in efficiency and the potential to improve certain services are clear; there is also a range of risks, including the tendency to overlook the environmental impact; it is essential to develop more sustainable technological solutions

### Responsibility, transparency and the governance of AI

* 102 - The use of AI is never a purely technical matter; it touches on rights, opportunities, status and freedom; can therefore give rise to new forms of exclusion.
* 103 - Entrusting an algorithm in practice with the power to select who is worthy or not is to hand over the task of redefining the boundaries of human possibilities; exclusion of the vulnerable becomes cloaked in a veneer of neutrality and objectivity
* 104 - A simple but compelling consequence: **we cannot consider AI to be morally neutral; every technical tool embodies choices and priorities**
* 105 - Responsibility must be clearly defined at every stage
* 106 - Calling for prudence, rigorous evaluation and even, at times, a slower pace in adopting AI does not mean opposing progress; instead, it is an exercise of responsible care for the human family; it is not enough to invoke ethics in the abstract; robust legal frameworks, independent oversight, informed users and a political system that does not abdicate its responsibility are required.
* 107 - We cannot be satisfied with merely calling for the moralization of machines; subjecting them to shared standards of social justice; **A more moral AI is not enough if that morality is determined by a few**; needed is a more active political involvement that is capable of slowing things down when everything is accelerating, and of protecting the opportunities for communities still to be able to participate and ask questions.
* 108 - **AI tends to amplify the power of those who already possess economic resources, expertise and access to data**; small but highly influential groups can shape information and consumption patterns, influence democratic processes and steer economic dynamics; **Communities and intermediary organizations must be able to contribute to discernment and oversight, and to manage data as a common or shared good**
* 109 - Social justice is not only a goal to be safeguarded after technologies are deployed, but a condition that must shape their very design from the outset.
* 110 - Our task today is not only ethical or technical. It is ecological in the deepest sense; AI is already an environment in which we are immersed, as well as a force with which we must engage; merely regulating it is insufficient; it must be disarmed, welcoming and accessible.
* 111 - Developers, therefore, bear a particular ethical and spiritual responsibility, for every design choice reflects a vision of humanity; developers are called to embed values in their projects with due seriousness

### What must not be lost

* 112 - Our central question: what does it mean to safeguard our humanity?; the pervasive technocratic paradigm threatens to normalize an anti-human vision; **When efficiency becomes the ultimate measure of value, human beings are tempted to see themselves as a project to be optimized rather than as persons called to relationship and communion.**
* 113 - In reality, elevating any single dimension of human existence to an absolute is always a mistake; intelligence, when absolutized, overshadows other essential dimensions of life; technical power, if left unbalanced, does not make us more capable; it makes us more isolated and more vulnerable to being dominated and excluded; when intelligence becomes self-referential, its true purpose of serving life and the human person is lost.
* 114 - **The quality of a civilization is measured not by the power of its means, but by the care it is able to offer**; The ability to care for one another is a fundamental dimension of our humanity, one that is learned and mastered through lived experience; **Technology can also support this mutual care without undermining human freedom and judgment.**

### Underlying narratives: transhumanism and posthumanism

* 115 - Certain currents of thought that interpret progress as surpassing the human condition, often grouped under the labels of transhumanism and posthumanism; ideological background present in some centers of technological power; foster enthusiasm for new technologies through a futuristic vision of an “enhanced human being” or “human-machine hybrid.”
* 117 - From the perspective of the Church’s Social Doctrine, the key issue is not the use of technology as such, but the vision that underlies it; scientific and technological advances, when detached from moral and social progress, end up turning against humanity; a clear distinction must be made. **It is one thing to integrate technology within a human-centered, relational vision; it is quite another to be guided by an outlook that devalues human limits and promises a purely technical form of “salvation.”**

### The limit, the heart and the grandeur of the human person

* 118 - Our relationship with life seems to be in crisis today. Everything that appears as a “limit” tends to be seen primarily as a defect to be corrected, rather than as a reality through which our humanity matures; humanity flourishes not despite limitations, but often through them.
* 119 - It is within our limitations that the following find a place: compassion, sincere concern for the needs of others, generosity, spiritual experience, worship of God; there are many moments when our limits become tangible; it is precisely in such moments that we can discover a new wisdom, tangibly experience the closeness of others and encounter the presence of the Lord.
* 120 - When limitations are experienced as inner suffering, human wisdom teaches us not to deny or suppress it, but to integrate it; Those who love and desire cannot avoid passing through trial and suffering; over the years, we carry within us lessons that leave their mark; it is only thanks to the interplay of these elements that the wonders of the soul occur within us, allowing us to sense the richness of our humanity.
* 121 - The moral corruption of our limitations ruins society and life; when persons dehumanize themselves and bring about tragedy, a small light continues to shine within humanity, one that can be rekindled
* 122 - Finitude, when truly accepted, does not diminish us but opens us to recognizing the face of God and others; because we experience limits, we can recognize the inviolable dignity of every person
* 123 - History does not appear solely as a record of human violence, but also as evidence that humanity is capable of creating institutions that protect our shared life; Moral progress almost always unfolds through a long and demanding journey; fragility of these achievements highlights how precious the responsibility is of those who initiate and sustain them.
* 124 - History can also change when individuals truly take the dignity of everyone seriously
* 125 - Goodness does not advance automatically, but requires the perseverance, memory and interior conversion necessary to begin anew, even after defeat.
* 126 - It is this intertwining of just institutions, credible witnesses and daily fidelity that sustains hope and provides clear direction for technological progress without allowing the heart to regress; **We can embrace the technological progress that alleviates suffering and unlocks new possibilities, provided that we do not abandon the very essence of our humanity, namely the capacity for relationship and love.**

### The authentic “more than human”: grace and Christian humanism

* 127 - For centuries, the Christian tradition has maintained that human beings are not confined by the boundaries of their own nature; rather, they are called to self-transcendence through their fulfillment in love.
* 128 - Pope Francis explained, “We become fully human when we become more than human, when we let God bring us beyond ourselves in order to attain the fullest truth of our being.”; what saves humanity is not enhanced self-sufficiency, but a relationship that liberates, a communion that transforms; technology that merely classifies and optimizes what already exists can, however unintentionally, become an obstacle to change and growth.

### Two cities and two loves

* 129 - The creative intelligence of humanity is a gift that can alleviate suffering and open up new possibilities, but it must remain ordered toward the common good, justice, the care of the vulnerable and creation; the true alternative is not between enthusiasm and fear, but between two paths of development: a progress that serves individuals and peoples, or a progress that subjects them to the mentality of power; a path of patient, shared reconstruction akin to the rebuilding of Jerusalem, or a new form of Babel
* 130 - Questioning this alternative path of progress and how we interpret and live it is ultimately a matter of examining our own hearts; Saint Augustine described human history as a struggle between two loves; on the one hand, the love of God and neighbor; on the other, the exclusive love of self; As throughout history, these two loves continue to contend for dominance in our hearts today.

## Chapter four - Safeguarding humanity at a time of transformation. Truth, work, freedom

Three concrete arenas. On truth: disinformation predates AI but has found an amplifier, and indifference to truth is named as a road to totalitarianism — truth being a common good rather than the property of whoever owns the platform. On work: automation is restructuring the whole thing, profit does not justify systematically destroying jobs, and innovation becomes either an ally of dignified work or an accelerator of injustice depending on conditions we choose. On freedom: platforms engineered to capture attention, a technocratic mentality that treats persons as resources to optimize, and data colonialism as one of the era's urgent moral challenges.

* 131 - We must focus on certain areas in which these transformations have particularly concrete, and at times tragic, consequences; rediscover truth as a common good; protect the dignity of work; safeguard freedom against all forms of dependence and commercialization.

### Truth and democracy

* 132 - Tools that could foster dialogue and participation are often used to construct distorted narratives and blur the boundaries between truth and falsehood; **Disinformation did not begin with AI, yet today it finds a powerful amplifier; This problem has both cultural and moral dimensions;** At the same time, truthful information does not arise from centralized or automated control; Only the shared pursuit of the veracity of facts, perceived as a common good, can provide a solid foundation for just communication.
* 133 - Those who command powerful technological and economic resources, along with substantial human capital for intervention, possess significant capabilities for influencing cultural change; pure power detached from truth, which subtly or overtly imposes what it wishes others to accept as true; a deeper and often unrecognized “sickness”; people believe that they can construct reality, and that whatever best suits their claims corresponds to what is true; In such a context, universally valid truths are no longer recognized; “If society is to have a future, it must respect the truth of our human dignity and submit to that truth.”
* 134 - **The search for truth is an essential element of democracy; when questions about what is true lose their appeal, democratic life is weakened; indifference to the truth leads to a descent into totalitarianism.**

### Communication and the collective imagination

* 135 - Communication “is not only the transmission of information, but it is also the creation of a culture.”; what originates online now becomes a part of people’s lives, especially of the youngest.
* 136 - Those who control digital platforms and means of communication have power that should be constantly guided by the pursuit of truth and respect for human dignity

### Toward an ecology of communication

* 137 - Our **first task is neither to demonize nor idolize technological tools, but to utilize them on the basis of a fundamental principle, namely that truth is a common good** and not the property of those with power or influence; we must promote an ecology of communication; public policy entails establishing norms; social and cultural aspects; the strengthening of intermediary organizations; families and schools; a new educational awareness; universities; the integration of knowledge
* 138 - Vigilance and transparency remain a grave responsibility for the Church

### An educational alliance for the digital age

* 139 - The pervasiveness of digital media fosters a culture of immediacy and hyper-stimulation
* 140 - Education, by contrast, is a long journey requiring patience; every technology shapes those who use it; speed and ease with which answers or summaries can be obtained risk extinguishing the desire to ask questions; **We must learn how to exercise restraint in the use of AI and to protect our young people from the promise of the perfect machine**
* 141 - Early and unsupervised exposure to digital devices and social media can negatively impact sleep, attention span, control of emotions and relationships; Having a personal mobile device at too early an age and using it without adult supervision can exacerbate young people’s vulnerabilities, foster addiction and expose them to isolation, bullying and cyberbullying, as well as to pressures to share intimate images or sensitive information.
* 142 - It is difficult for parents by themselves to resist the influence of business models that monetize attention and time; it is essential to form an alliance among policy-makers, educational institutions and families; At the same time, it is also necessary to teach children, adolescents and young people how to recognize manipulation, defend their dignity and respect that of others in digital environments.

### The central role of schools

* 145 - The advance of information technologies and AI is rapidly rendering curricula obsolete that were designed for a different era.
* 146 - Without careful attention, an educational system lacking in a love for truth may emerge, in which an incessant flow of information replaces the essential exercise of research, reflection and discernment.
* 147 - The Church’s Social Doctrine invites families, schools, Christian communities and public institutions to form a renewed educational alliance; fundamental principles are translated into educational goals, including teaching students a sense of moderation and limits; recognition of the rights of others and of future generations to enjoy the goods that are either provided for us or made available by human ingenuity; freedom and responsibility; and a sense of transcendence and the common good; schools are called to **offer that which the digital sphere by itself cannot provide, namely a shared time for learning and developing trustworthy relationships.**

### The value of work

* 148 - It is through their work that individuals develop many dimensions of their existence; we contribute to the progress of society and the common good, put to good use the capabilities we have received, improve and beautify the world, support our families, engage in cooperative relationships and, through listening and dialogue, learn to build together something that no one could achieve alone.
* 149 - Work is not simply an instrument; it expresses and enhances the dignity of our lives.
* 150 - **The convergence of automation, robotics and AI is rapidly transforming the very structure of work**; The need to keep up with the pace of technology can erode workers’ sense of agency and stifle the innovative abilities they are expected to bring to their work; **it is necessary to design systems that are centered on the human person** and not solely on performance.

### The problem of unemployment

* 152 - It is certainly desirable for technology to relieve humans of arduous, repetitive or dangerous tasks; **The irreplaceable role of the individual must remain the general rule; the pursuit of greater profits cannot justify choices that systematically sacrifice jobs; the economic order must remain subordinate to human dignity and the common good.**
* 153 - Given the inequality that characterizes our world, the spread of AI and computational systems produces varied effects in different places; solutions must be sought at national and local levels; We need adaptive tools, including well-structured models, local initiatives, progressive redistribution and new rights of access to essential goods.
* 154 - Work remains a fundamental dimension of the human experience; a means of sustenance; a context for expression, relationships and contributing to the community; A society that guarantees employment to only a small fraction of the population risks exposing many to human and cultural impoverishment; it undermines the foundations of a just and stable social peace; in those parts of the world where work tends to diminish or change radically, we must rethink the nature of work and its connection to citizenship, ensuring that unemployment does not jeopardize social participation.
* 155 - New collaborative efforts are needed among political leaders, labor organizations, the business world and the scientific community in order to develop rapidly adequate shared regulations and protections, including at the international level; without bold decisions, the prospect of greater poverty and inequality looms large
* 156 - It is not enough to react only when jobs disappear; we must oversee the transformation in advance; a viable path is, first of all, to establish social criteria for innovation; every introduction of automation and AI should be accompanied by verifiable measures to protect the employment, retraining and participation of workers; Second, we need proactive policies that make continuous training and professional transitions accessible to all; Finally, there needs to be a corporate commitment to include quality and dignity of work among its indicators of success; **When these conditions are present, innovation can serve as an ally of safer, more creative and dignified work; without them, innovation tends to become an accelerator of injustice.**

### An economy that values dignity

* 157 - Economic freedom is not absolute; it must always be measured against the common good and the dignity of every person; the creation of dignified, valuable jobs is an essential part of its proper service to society.
* 158 - Economic models that exalt efficiency and individual success often view investment in disadvantaged people or in those with slower development paths as useless or inconvenient; In reality, **a just society requires a vigilant State and civil institutions that are capable of overcoming the singular mentality of efficiency, and of ensuring that resources, creative solutions and regulations favor the most vulnerable.**
* 160 - The financial intermediation sector, “when operating without the necessary anthropological and moral foundations, has not only produced manifest abuses and injustice, but also demonstrated a capacity to create systemic and worldwide economic crisis.”; Finance for its own sake is fundamentally different from finance aimed at the development, creation and evolution of work.
* 161 - While the world’s wealth has grown in absolute terms, it is increasingly concentrated in fewer hands; To think that new technologies will automatically benefit everyone is to ignore the evidence. Unless transformations at the design stage prioritize the prevention of new and further disparities, technological progress will inevitably produce structural inequalities.
* 162 - Just laws and methods of redistribution are certainly necessary; However, the pursuit of social justice should not be considered a separate issue that follows only after the production of wealth; justice concerns every phase of economic activity
* 163 - Politics has the task of orientating economies and technologies to the common good; Since many economic decisions transcend national borders, there is also a need for international cooperation; The interdependence between peace and development remains applicable today, for prosperity contributes to building and reinforcing peace only if it is widespread, inclusive and sustainable.
* 164 - In practical terms, in the age of AI and robotics, this means adopting certain criteria: transparency and accountability, inclusion and access, measures to ensure equity; these criteria do not constitute a curb on innovation; instead they make it civilized and humane.

### Families and young people: the social conditions for hope

* 165 - The family is a primary social good; it is the first environment in which all persons develop their potential, become aware of their dignity and learn the earliest forms of truth and goodness; family is the fundamental and irreplaceable cell of every community organization.
* 166 - The family, however, is a fragile social good immediately affected by the economic and technological transformations; it requires cultural, juridical and economic support; it may seem advantageous to reduce labor costs, yet in the long term this undermines the very foundations of social coexistence.
* 167 - For young people, job insecurity is particularly devastating; work is not merely a source of income but a crucial sphere in which identity is formed, friendships and relationships are forged, practical responsibilities are learned and one’s vocation is discerned; When access to work is hindered, many young people find the path to their human and professional fulfilment blocked.
* 168 - This gives rise to a specific public responsibility. The State has the duty to support business activity by fostering conditions favorable to employment; since it is a primary good for families and for society; we need a political creativity that will promote “work” and place the family and coming generations at the center
* 169 - Supporting families and young people in this transition requires choices that make stability feasible; labor policies need to promote continuity and the quality of employment; measures are needed to ensure a healthy way of living; it is essential to invest in accessible education and retraining; social ties must be supported

### Dependencies and societal control

* 170 - We must now **consider the impact of the digital revolution on human freedom, addressing risks to both the mental health of individuals and broader social challenges**; platforms and services are often designed to capture users’ time and attention, exploiting their vulnerabilities and weakening their inner freedom; There is an urgent need to promote technologies that strengthen interior freedom by fostering education in digital sobriety and the protection of minors
* 171 - A further risk is the social control made possible by the massive collection of data and use of algorithmic systems; freedom in the digital age calls for clear rules, transparency, the possibility of recourse and proportionate limits on the use of intrusive technologies
* 172 - **At the root of these problems lies a technocratic and post-humanist mentality that tends to regard the human person as an object to be manipulated or a resource to be optimized; What prevails is efficiency, rather than respect for freedom and human dignity; This troubling prospect becomes all the more serious when combined with technological tools that exponentially increase the capacity for control and selection.**

### Breaking the chains of new forms of slavery

* 173 - A significant part of the digital economy’s functioning relies on the silent work of millions of people engaged in essential yet largely unseen activities, such as data labeling, model training and content moderation; If technology promises emancipation, yet produces new forms of global subordination, it stands in contradiction to the fundamental principle of human dignity.
* 177 - The memory of past complicity and blindness in the face of the injustice of slavery becomes a call to vigilance.
* 178 - Even **today, colonialism assumes new forms. It no longer dominates only bodies, but appropriates data**; health data, epidemiological profiles, genetic maps and demographic information; **one of the most urgent moral challenges of our time: to ensure that shared knowledge becomes a true common good rather than an instrument of dominance**; requires restoring to individuals not only the data that describes them, but also the ability to decide how it is used, by whom and for whose benefit.

### A shared responsibility

* 180 - If technology becomes the ultimate criterion, the human person risks being reduced to data; If, however, technology is integrated with a wise perspective, it can become an instrument of growth, justice and fraternity.
* 181 - The Social Doctrine of the Church calls for a shared responsibility; Only in this way can innovation genuinely serve integral human development, rather than becoming a source of exclusion and dominance.

## Chapter five - The culture of power and the civilization of love

The bleakest chapter and the most insistent. A culture of power is normalizing war, the military-industrial link produces an "armed nation," wars are far easier to start than to stop, and autonomous weapons make force more feasible and less controlled — for which three criteria are set: identifiable responsibility, a moral timeframe that speed cannot override, and protection of civilians. Underneath it sits a crisis of multilateralism and a false realism insisting there is no alternative. The answer offered is unglamorous: refuse resignation, refuse neutrality in the face of atrocity, keep a realism that still intends to change the world, and accept that encounter with the stranger makes war harder to imagine.

* 182 - We must now turn our attention to the yet more tragic issue of war; there is a risk that **technology, detached from ethics and responsibility, will render decisions about life and death more rapid and impersonal**, and will present the use of force as an immediate and viable option.
* 183 - **The digital revolution is changing the nature of conflict. Alongside conventional warfare, there are hybrid forms such as cyberattacks, information manipulation, campaigns of influence and the automation of strategic decisions. AI acts as an accelerating factor**
* 185 - There seems to be no limit to the race — driven by a dehumanizing ambition — to develop evermore powerful technologies or to secure control over them; we can also glimpse a great part of humanity that is striving to remain human and working to build the holy city of coexistence and peace; It is this prospect of commitment, this construction site of hope, that we call the “civilization of love.”

### The civilization of love in the digital age

* 186 - When Saint Paul VI coined the phrase “the civilization of love” in the midst of the Cold War, he envisioned a social order in which justice and charity are intertwined and love becomes the guiding principle
* 187 - This insight proves even more fundamental in the current context of digital transformation; the growing interdependence between peoples; the common good is taking on an increasingly universal dimension; **This is the guiding principle for technological processes: it is not enough for artificial intelligence to make us more efficient or connected; it must also serve to build a universal human family**

### The culture of power

* 188 - **A culture of power is taking hold; the ability to dominate tends to dictate the agenda; common good of humanity is relegated to the background; grows by normalizing war, pursuing ever-greater military power, taking advantage of the crisis of multilateralism and fueling a false realism that insists that there is no alternative.**

### The normalization of war

* 190 - We are witnessing a real paradigm shift in public discourse and in decisions regarding rearmament, with a troubling revival of war as an instrument of international politics; Public opinion is gradually being shaped and conditioned by polarizing media narratives
* 191 - We are also witnessing a disconcerting loss of historical memory
* 192 - Media and digital dimensions are adding new and decisive elements; fragmented information environments and algorithms that reward conflict can magnify polarization and resentment; war is not only fought, but also culturally conditioned through simplistic narratives; humanity is slipping into a violent culture of power, where peace no longer appears as a responsibility to be taken on, but as a fragile interval between conflicts; The use of force, violence and weapons reflects a relational poverty that always has disastrous consequences

### Force without limits

* 193 - **The growth of the military-industrial complex; the close link between economic interests, the military apparatus and political decisions produces an “armed nation,” in which war appears as a natural extension of politics**
* 194 - Military arsenals are receiving renewed attention; widespread yet erroneous belief that nuclear deterrence is an indispensable prerequisite for security.
* 195 - **It is much easier to start a war than to stop it**, and yet, discussion on conflict prevention remains tragically marginal.
* 196 - The situation is further destabilized by the presence of new armed operatives; Often these groups intertwine vague ideological motivations with concrete economic interests, transforming war into a “way of life”; the objective is no longer a definitive victory, but the perpetuation of conflict as a source of power and income.

### Weapons and artificial intelligence

* 197 - The unceasing development of weapons systems, particularly those involving AI; **growing ease with which autonomous weapons systems can be deployed makes war more “feasible” and less subject to human control; development and use of AI in warfare must be subject to the most rigorous ethical constraints**
* 198 - Moral judgment cannot be reduced to calculation; it is not permissible to entrust lethal or otherwise irreversible decisions to artificial systems; AI does not remove the intrinsic inhumanity of conflict
* 199 - It is not enough to invoke a generic type of ethics. **Concrete criteria for discernment must be established; the first such criterion concerns personal responsibility;** the chain of responsibility must be identifiable and verifiable; **the second criterion pertains to the moral timeframe**; speed and efficiency should never be the supreme motivating force for the irreversible decisions made in the context of war; **the third criterion is the identification and protection of civilians**; Target selection and the use of force must not confuse combatants and non-combatants
* 200 - There are certain non-negotiable requirements; **all systems used in a war setting must guarantee the possibility of retracing and reconstructing decision-making processes**; decision to use lethal force cannot be delegated to opaque or automated processes; establish a shared framework — also at the international level — in order to curb the technological arms race and ensure robust protection for civilians

### The crisis of multilateralism

* 201 - **The culture of power also stems from the crisis of the multilateral system; we are regressing from the significant turning point of the twentieth century**; rather than automatically generating unity and peace, globalization has provoked fundamentalist, identity-based and nationalistic reactions.
* 202 - What has also re-emerged is the temptation to forge a collective identity in opposition to an enemy
* 203 - In this context, peacebuilding has been relegated to a secondary role.

### A supposed political realism

* 204 - **We live at a time of significant spiritual and cultural blindness**; increased military spending is presented as the only response to an uncertain future or perceived threats. Meanwhile, the real cost falls on the poorest, who see resources for healthcare, education and social services being reduced.
* 205 - At the core of these issues is a false realism, based not only on the prevailing mentality of force, but on the cultural and anthropological belief that war is an inevitable part of human nature; the concern is no longer the search for peace, but rather how and when to take military action; what is truly irresponsible is Realpolitik; peace is neither a naïve hope nor merely the absence of war; instead, it is always possible as the fruit of justice and charity.
* 206 - **In such a climate, nihilism and pragmatism become intertwined; Religious extremism and identity-based fanaticism ally themselves with irrational economic policies, while politics often turns to misinformation and ridiculing opponents; diversity is increasingly perceived as a threat**
* 207 - This, then, is the fertile ground for new wars that are perhaps even more dangerous than those of the past; When people come to believe that nothing is genuinely true and that principles are hollow words, then the fuse in their hearts is lit for new eruptions of intolerance and aggression.
* 208 - When a culture normalizes and justifies conflict, a dangerous pathway opens up

### Building the civilization of love

* 211 - Grace does not magically eliminate conflict, but instead it inspires active resistance to evil and an astonishing creativity in doing good.

### We can all do our part

* 212 - **A subtle temptation may emerge, namely the thought that the problems are too big and we are too small; This is a polite form of resignation; no one is without responsibility.**
* 213 - **The civilization of love will not arise from a single or spectacular gesture, but from the sum total of small and steadfast acts** of fidelity that serve as a bulwark against dehumanization; five paths toward daily and public responsibility: the need to disarm words, building peace through justice, adopting the perspective of victims, cultivating a healthy realism and reviving dialogue and multilateralism.

### The need to disarm words

* 214 - The first contribution we can make toward a more humane civilization is to be mindful of our words.

### Building peace through justice

* 215 - All of us, at every level, can contribute to building the foundation of peace, which is justice.

### Adopting the perspective of victims

* 216 - **In some conflicts, it is unjust to remain neutral; When we witness the bombing of civilians, attacks on hospitals, schools or vital infrastructure, and violence that affects children, we are confronted with scandals that wound humanity itself.**
* 217 - In a particular way, the Church can be a place of living memory for victims.

### Cultivating a healthy realism

* 218 - We are in need of a healthy realism that avoids both political idealism and cynicism; **Authentic realism does not give up on changing the world; It does not reduce politics to morality; neither does it surrender to violence. Instead, it seeks viable paths for making peace more than a mere word**

### Reviving dialogue

* 219 - In order to build the civilization of love, we must engage in dialogue
* 220 - **If we experience authentic encounters with others, with those who are different, strangers and migrants, it becomes much more difficult even to imagine war.**
* 221 - At the political level, there is an urgent need to shift from the “culture of power” to a genuine “culture of negotiation,” in which dialogue and diplomacy become the standard means of resolving conflicts.
* 222 - Those who make history are the peacemakers, not those who sow seeds of suffering. Our neighbors are not first our enemies, but our fellow human beings
* 223 - In rejecting the mindset of violence, interreligious dialogue plays a decisive role; Whereas those who use the name of God to legitimize terrorism, violence or war betray his true nature; there is no room for “sanctified hatred.”

### The necessity of diplomacy and multilateralism

* 224 - **Every ounce of humility and patience should be employed in order to nurture even the faintest signs of goodwill**
* 225 - **Cyberspace too has become a battleground.** Cyberattacks, data manipulation and campaigns of influence, orchestrated with the help of AI, can destabilize entire countries even before open armed conflict erupts; **diplomacy must be capable of operating effectively in this new environment**
* 226 - International organizations, particularly the United Nations, are essential instruments for promoting a civilization of love; the crisis of convictions and values that also concerns the ethical foundations of nations makes it more difficult to direct multilateralism toward the true common good.

### Praying and hoping

* 228 - These avenues for exercising responsibility are sustained by prayer, and in turn nourish prayer; **Let us never tire of praying for peace and of committing ourselves to achieving it in our relationships and in society.**

## Conclusion

A four-part program — be faithful to the truth, invest in education, cultivate relationships, love justice and peace. The claim underwriting it is that no computational system, however sophisticated, can produce a heart that gives itself or a conscience that discerns good from evil. What follows is practical: engage the digital world in a human way, protect places where physical presence still matters, and treat every technical and economic decision as an occasion to ask whether AI is spreading justice or concentrating power.

* 229 - At the end of this reflection, I would like to propose a sober yet demanding program of Christian life with which we can navigate this epochal change in the light of the Gospel.

### The Word became flesh

* 230 - Our world is filled with attempts to seize control of markets and spheres of influence; Yet our hearts yearn for an approach that is wise and benevolent
* 231 - The gift of peace enters into the world in a paradoxical way. It does so through the power to become children of God, and is awakened when we allow ourselves to be moved by the tears of the little ones, the fragility of the elderly, the silence of victims and the struggle of those who fight against the evil they do not wish to commit.
* 232 - In the promises of transhumanism and some posthumanist currents of thought, we recognize a yearning that is of concern to us, namely the need for a fuller life; There is no moment or human situation that is not worthy of God; The future of humanity, therefore, finds its standard in the ability to welcome this divine way of drawing near, of sharing the burden of the world, of transforming relationships from within; What saves humanity is the divine love that descends into the most fragile point of our history and renews it from within.
* 233 - I invite everyone to **contemplate the grandeur of humanity that shines a light also on the era of AI; No computational system, however sophisticated, can create a heart that gives itself, or a conscience that discerns good from evil.**

### One body in Christ

* 234 - **The spirituality that we need is a Eucharistic spirituality, that is, a spirituality of ecclesial unity in love.**
* 235 - In the Eucharist we find a visible manifestation of the reality that we “are the Church of Christ… though many and diverse, we are one”; The Eucharist opens us to justice and sharing; **called to make visible a different paradigm, one that preserves human connections, gives a voice to the invisible and ensures that processes are aimed at respecting people’s dignity.**

### The construction site of our time

* 236 - As I mentioned at the beginning of this reflection, **the task of building in our time must place our relationship with God at its center**; At the end of this reflection, the plan for a civilization of love can be seen more clearly; In this task, we are called to assume an active role, without taking refuge in spiritual sentimentality or retreating into our own little worlds. **We must be faithful to the truth, invest in education, cultivate relationships and love justice and peace.**
* 237 - Let us remain faithful to the truth! Living amid incessant flows of information, opinions and images, we know how easy it can be to influence decisions and preferences through increasingly sophisticated algorithms; imperative to cultivate hearts that love the truth, prefer what is right despite the most appealing content and pursue wisdom rather than immediate results; cultivate what Pope Francis called a “situated anthropocentrism,” which recognizes the human being as a creature embedded in a network of relationships with other living beings and with all of creation. **Fidelity to the truth requires integrating the possibilities offered by technology within a framework marked by wisdom, which is capable of safeguarding both the dignity of each person and the future of our common home.**
* 238 - Invest in education; **We all need to learn how to engage with the digital world in a human way**; Teaching new generations that technological evolution does not follow a predetermined path, but can be guided by personal and collective responsibility, constitutes one of the most valuable services to the common good.
* 239 - Cultivate relationships!; digital culture multiplies connections and offers new opportunities for interaction; yet, **the human heart retains an irrevocable need for genuine closeness; cherish places and times where physical presence remains crucial**
* 240 - Love justice and peace!; same technologies that facilitate communication and access to resources can also support models that exploit the most vulnerable; **Every technical or economic decision should include spiritual discernment and be an opportunity for assessing whether the advances in AI are promoting justice and participation or concentrating wealth and power in the hands of a select few**; practical ways of fostering fairness, participation and care for creation must be found.
* 241 - Like Nehemiah, we too are **called to unite listening and courage, prayer and responsibility, so that, even when a technocratic mentality or partisan interests seem to prevail, the human city may become a more fitting place to live.**

### The song of hope: the Magnificat

* 244 - The Blessed Virgin Mary not only teaches us to recognize God’s invisible work, but also directs our gaze to “the points at which humanity is broken and the world becomes distorted”
* 245 - With the same faith as Mary, **let us become “weavers of hope” in our world, sharing who we are and what we have**; In the humble fidelity of daily life, even the era of AI can become a time in which the Holy Spirit brings about the civilization of love in our lives; the Lord continues to make all things new and offers every era the possibility of becoming part of salvation history; Mother of Christ, **guide our steps through this time of change and preserve in each of us true faith; so that we may bear witness to the grandeur of humanity**
